# Overview

Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is a highly potent mycotoxin produced by certain species of *Aspergillus* fungi, particularly *Aspergillus flavus* and *Aspergillus parasiticus*. It is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), indicating its carcinogenic potential in humans. AFB1 is a significant contaminant in various food and feed products, posing serious health risks to both humans and animals.

# Evidence and Details

## Toxicological Relevance

Aflatoxin B1 is one of the most potent naturally occurring carcinogens, with a strong affinity for liver tissue. Its carcinogenic properties are primarily attributed to its ability to form DNA adducts after metabolic activation by cytochrome P450 enzymes. These adducts can lead to mutations in critical genes, such as the tumor suppressor gene p53, which is strongly associated with the development of liver cancer.

## Metabolic Activation

AFB1 is metabolically activated in the liver to a reactive epoxide intermediate, AFB1-8,9-epoxide, which binds covalently to guanine residues in DNA. This process is facilitated by enzymes such as CYP3A4 and CYP1A2. The resulting DNA adducts can interfere with normal cellular processes, leading to mutations and potentially cancer.

## Exposure Assessment

AFB1 contamination is a global concern, particularly in regions with warm and humid climates that favor the growth of *Aspergillus* species. Common sources of contamination include:
- **Rice:** Often contaminated during storage under high humidity.
- **Maize:** Susceptible to contamination both in the field and during storage.
- **Peanuts:** Highly prone to AFB1 contamination due to their protein-rich content and storage conditions.

The presence of AFB1 in food products is regulated by various international bodies, including the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), which set maximum permissible limits to ensure food safety.

## Health Effects

### Acute Toxicity
Exposure to high levels of AFB1 can result in acute toxicity, manifesting as liver damage, jaundice, and, in severe cases, death. The symptoms of acute aflatoxicosis include:
- Nausea and vomiting
- Abdominal pain
- Liver enlargement
- Jaundice
- In severe cases, liver failure

### Chronic Toxicity
Chronic exposure to lower levels of AFB1 is associated with an increased risk of liver cancer. The carcinogenic effects of AFB1 are particularly pronounced in individuals with underlying liver conditions, such as hepatitis B or C, due to the compounded stress on liver function.

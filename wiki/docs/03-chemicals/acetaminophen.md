# Overview

Acetaminophen, also known as paracetamol or APAP, is a widely used over-the-counter analgesic and antipyretic medication. It is commonly used to relieve pain and reduce fever. Despite its widespread use and general perception of safety, acetaminophen is the leading cause of acute liver failure in the United States and other countries, primarily due to overdosescit-001.

# Key Claims

## Claim 1: Mechanism of Action

Acetaminophen functions as an antipyretic and analgesic with a mechanism distinct from typical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications. It selectively inhibits cyclooxygenase in the brain, effectively alleviating fever and pain. Additionally, it may inhibit prostaglandin synthesis in the central nervous system and directly affect the hypothalamus to produce an antipyretic effectcit-001.

## Claim 2: Toxicological Relevance

Acetaminophen toxicity is the leading cause of acute liver failure. Over 60 million Americans use acetaminophen weekly, often unknowingly, due to its presence in various combination products, particularly those containing opioids and diphenhydramine. If left untreated, acetaminophen toxicity can lead to fatal hepatic necrosiscit-001.

## Claim 3: Hepatotoxicity

Acetaminophen-induced liver injury is a significant concern. Studies have shown that combining in vitro data from 3D cultured HepaRG cells with physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBTK) models can predict chemical-induced liver injury. The APAP concentration inducing mitochondrial damage in 3D culture was more similar to the liver C max in clinical APAP-induced liver injury than that obtained via 2D culturescit-002.

## Claim 4: Treatment of Overdose

Acetylcysteine is the critical antidote for acetaminophen poisoning and is most effective if administered within the first 8 hours after an acute overdose. Patients with suspected chronic acetaminophen poisoning should receive acetylcysteine as soon as possible. The Rumack-Matthew nomogram is used to predict the risk of acute toxicity by correlating the time of ingestion with the serum acetaminophen concentrationcit-001.

## Claim 5: Metabolism

Acetaminophen is metabolized primarily in the liver. It undergoes phase II metabolism to form non-toxic conjugates, but a small fraction is metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes to form N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), a toxic metabolite. NAPQI is responsible for liver damage in cases of acetaminophen overdosecit-003.

# Evidence and Details

## Toxicological Studies

Acetaminophen toxicity is primarily due to the formation of NAPQI, which depletes glutathione and leads to oxidative stress and liver damage. In vitro models, such as 3D cultured HepaRG cells, have been used to study the mechanisms of acetaminophen-induced liver injury. These models provide a more accurate representation of liver physiology compared to traditional 2D culturescit-002.

## Clinical Studies

Clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of acetylcysteine in treating acetaminophen overdose. Early administration of acetylcysteine is crucial for preventing liver failure and reducing the need for liver transplantation. The Rumack-Matthew nomogram is a widely used tool for assessing the risk of liver injury based on serum acetaminophen levels and the time of ingestioncit-001.

## Metabolism Studies

Studies comparing cytochrome P450 activity and mRNA expression in canine versus human hepatocytes after acetaminophen exposure have shown species-specific differences. These differences highlight the importance of accurate experimental models for predicting toxicity and adverse drug reactions in humanscit-003.
